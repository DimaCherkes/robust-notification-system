# 1.1 Context and Motivation

According to Google's 2022 *State of DevOps* report, the primary driving factor in the evolution of modern software is **reliability**. Over the past few years, the tech industry has realized that simply delivering features is no longer enough; companies are now investing massive amounts of time and resources into ensuring their solutions are fault-tolerant. But what does it actually mean for a system to be reliable in the real world?

To understand this, we have to look at the business impact of software. Imagine standing at the front of a long line in a supermarket. You tap your credit card to pay, and the transaction is declined due to a banking system error. If this happens once, it is frustrating; if it happens twice, you will likely switch to a competitor's bank. For a core banking system, the acceptable margin of error—often called the **Service Level Agreement (SLA) Corridor**—is virtually zero. A transaction either goes through, or it does not. The business must pay whatever it costs to ensure that this specific microservice never fails, because failure directly results in lost customers and lost money. 

Conversely, imagine you are scrolling through a social media application and you "like" a photo. If the microservice responsible for processing likes is currently overloaded, the application does not need to crash. The frontend can simply show you the red heart animation locally and send the data to the server later when the load decreases. In this case, the SLA corridor is wide. 

This brings us to a crucial realization: **reliability costs money, and it is not a binary state.** Every additional "nine" of availability (e.g., moving from 99.9% to 99.99% uptime) results in an exponential increase in infrastructure and development costs. Therefore, modern engineering is not about making every single component indestructible. It is about understanding which parts of the system are critical to the business and ensuring that when the less-critical parts inevitably fail, the user experience is preserved. 

This concept is known as **Graceful Degradation**. As industry experts often highlight, if a secondary service fails, the user should not be met with a broken screen or a "500 Internal Server Error." For instance, if an e-commerce application's geolocation service goes offline, it shouldn't crash the entire website. Instead, the application should gracefully degrade by identifying the user as a "standard" user and displaying default content (e.g., showing standard shipping times for Bratislava instead of personalized data for Prešov). The user still gets what they came for: the ability to browse and buy products.

The motivation for this thesis stems from these very challenges. Designing a system that knows how to fail gracefully—specifically a Robust Notification System—requires a paradigm shift. We must move away from hoping our systems never crash, and instead design them with the assumption that they will.
